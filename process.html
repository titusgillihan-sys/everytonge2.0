How it works

Four plain-English ideas explain the whole tool.

1. In tonal languages, pitch is part of the word

In English, you can say a word high, low, rising, or flat, and it's still the same word — you've just changed your tone of voice. Tonal languages — spoken across much of West Africa, Southeast Asia, and East Asia — don't work that way: the pitch pattern of a syllable is part of the word. Mandarin is the concrete example used throughout this demo, since it has the tone-marked dictionary data to check today:

妈mā1st — flatmother
麻má2nd — risinghemp
马mǎ3rd — dippinghorse
骂mà4th — fallingto scold

Same sound, four different pitches, four unrelated words.

2. What breaks a sung line is contrary motion

A tune assigns every syllable a fixed note. When a hymn is translated into Mandarin but kept on its original Western melody, nobody adjusted the tune to fit the new words. The damage is not done by any single note being "wrong" — it is done by the melody moving opposite to the way the speaking voice moves between two syllables (Ladd & Kirby, 2020).

So the check is never "does this tone match this note." It is always about the transition between two neighbouring syllables: where the previous tone ends, where this one begins, and whether the tune goes with that movement or against it.

ToneShapeStarts / ends
1stflat / highhigh → high
2ndrisingmid → high
3rddipping / lowlow → lowest
4thfallinghigh → lowest
neutral—takes its pitch from context; never flagged

A 4th tone ends at the bottom of the range; a following 4th tone starts back at the top. The voice therefore leaps up between them. If the melody descends across that pair, tune and language are pulling in opposite directions — that is a flag. If both move the same way, or either is flat, there is no conflict, whatever the individual tones happen to be.

Two details matter for getting this right. The first syllable of a line has nothing before it, so it can never be flagged. And a 3rd tone before another 3rd tone is spoken as a rising 2nd tone — 你好 is written nǐ hǎo and said ní hǎo — so the analysis runs on the tone actually sung, not the one the dictionary lists.

3. Meaning is a separate gate, not part of the score

Once a clash is found, the obvious move is to swap in a word that sings better. The obvious move is also how you quietly destroy a translation: it is always possible to improve a singability score by saying something else.

So meaning is a gate, not a tiebreaker. Every candidate replacement carries a meaning distance (0 = interchangeable, 1 = a nuance shift, 2 = a different claim). Anything over the budget is removed before ranking begins, and ranking only ever orders what survived. A candidate can never buy its way past the gate by scoring well.

Set phrases are gated structurally too. 恩典 ("grace") is one word, so swapping a single syllable of it produces gibberish rather than a synonym — the tool refuses to offer one and says so, instead of inventing a plausible-looking replacement.

When nothing survives the gate, the honest answer is that the words cannot be fixed — and the tool offers the alternative that costs no meaning at all: move the note instead.

4. The check itself is see-through — not a black box

Tones come from a small pinyin/tone dictionary (formatted the way the public CC-CEDICT dataset records it). The melody is a plain list of "up / down / same" steps. Comparing them is arithmetic on two numbers, and the same function runs on every page of this site, so nothing is hidden or hand-waved. Characters outside the dictionary are marked unknown, never guessed.

Candidate replacements are generated from an inspectable substitution lexicon and ranked by how much they reduce the line's total contrary motion. No model is called at any point.
